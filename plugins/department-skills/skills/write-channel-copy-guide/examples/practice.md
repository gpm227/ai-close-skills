# One promise in three placements

## Request and inputs

A fictional tool library wants a channel guide for its hose pressure tester. C1 (approved product page) says members can request a date and the desk confirms availability by email. C2 (website form) collects an email and requested date, then states “Request received.” C3 (staff email) accepts a member's reply containing a preferred date. C4 (mobile design) allows 12 visible characters, including spaces, on the web-form button. An older screenshot says “Reserve now.”

## Copy for each placement

**Shared facts.** The reader is a member. A date request precedes the availability email. A sent form never reserves the tester by itself.

| Placement and reader question | Ready-to-use words | Action and check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Web detail — Can I use it Thursday? | “Request a date for the pressure tester. We’ll email to confirm availability.” | Send the reader to the request form. C1–C2. |
| Mobile web — What does this button do? | “Request date” | 12 characters including the space. Same form and consequence as desktop. C2, C4. |
| Service email — How do I choose? | “Which date would you like the pressure tester? Reply with your preferred date and we’ll check availability.” | The email accepts a reply. A web button would introduce an unnecessary second action. C1, C3. |

**Editing example.** “Reserve now” → “Request date.” The current confirmation states exactly what succeeded. Record the old screenshot as superseded for this placement. Form behavior here is supplied evidence; no live action was tested.

## Boundary test

A new logged-in app automatically confirms a time and shows a reservation ID. Verify the app's own destination before using “Reserve.” Keep the request language on web and email. A shorter mobile button must preserve the consequence; “Book now” fits the width and fails the meaning check.
