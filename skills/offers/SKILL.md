# Offers

You build the thing being sold: what is included, what is excluded, what it costs, what happens if it fails, and what the buyer does next. Copy cannot rescue an offer that asks too much for too little.

## Bring

| What | Minimum to start | What makes the answer good |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Delivery reality | What you actually do, and how long it takes | Cost and hours per delivery, current capacity |
| Buyer's alternative | What they use now | What that costs them, in money or effort |
| Price | Current or intended price | The floor you will not go below, and why |
| Proof | Any prior result | Who it was for, and whether it generalises |

## What comes back

1. The offer, written as a table: included, excluded, term, price, risk reversal, next step.
2. Two sentences that make it legible: one for the buyer who is ready, one for the one comparing.
3. The exclusions, stated out loud, because they are what makes the promise credible.
4. A test of the risk reversal: what it costs you if every buyer takes it.

## The trap, and the check

**The trap.** Discounting a positioning problem. When an offer stalls, the cheapest move looks like a lower price or a longer bonus list. Both leave the buyer's actual objection untouched, which is usually "I do not believe this will work for someone like me" or "I cannot tell what I am committing to". The second version of the trap is a guarantee written to sound generous that the business could not honour ten times in a row.

**Check.** Two tests. First, can you deliver exactly what is written, at that price, on that timeline, with the team you have this month? Second, if every buyer claimed the guarantee, could you pay it and stay solvent? If either answer is no, the offer is a promise, not an offer.

## How to do the job

1. **Name the unit of value.** A project, a month, a seat, an outcome. Ambiguity here causes every later argument about scope.
2. **Write the exclusions first.** What is not included, what the buyer must do, what makes it not work. An offer without exclusions reads as a claim.
3. **Price against the alternative, not against your costs.** The buyer compares you to the cost of the problem or to the other quote. Your costs set the floor, not the price.
4. **Choose the risk reversal you can afford.** Money back, a smaller first step, a fixed scope, or a delivered artifact. Pick the one that addresses the real fear, which is usually wasted time rather than wasted money.
5. **Remove one thing.** Most stalled offers are overloaded. Cut the element nobody asks about and see whether the objection changes.
6. **Write the next step as a decision, not a demo.** "Book a call" is not a decision. "Send your last invoice and we will price the scope" is.
7. **Name it.** A named offer is easier to refer to, compare and remember. The name should describe the unit of value, not the benefit.

## Questions that change the answer

1. What do buyers compare this to, and what does that cost them?
2. What is the most common reason a deal stalls after the price is known?
3. If a buyer took the guarantee today, what would it cost you?
